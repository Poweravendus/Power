Which rules actually do work?

Each rule run alone, then the full set with each rule removed in turn — all on the identical stop and target. The dashed line is the do-nothing baseline: every day in the dataset, same exits. A rule earns its place only if it beats that line by more than its interval.

Average return per trade, with 95% confidence intervals

Dashed line = all-days baseline (+0.64%, n=11,796) · p = share of random same-size samples that beat this subset

Volume is the only rule that clearly works. On its own it produces 1,692 trades at +1.86% — nearly three times the baseline, with an interval that never touches it (p < 0.001). The 3–5% close band is a real but smaller effect (+1.23%, p = 0.03).

Open = Low and gap-up contribute nothing. Open = Low returns +0.52% against a +0.64% baseline — below average, p = 0.62. Gap-up returns +0.59% and matches 6,807 of 11,796 rows, so it barely filters at all: p = 0.69.

Regime

Every filter loses in a falling market and wins in a rising one

Splitting the window in half is the clearest test of whether any of this is a signal. The universe fell 7.0% in the first half and rose 51.1% in the second. Every filter — and the do-nothing baseline — flips sign with it.

Average return per trade, first half vs second half

Split at 19 Feb 2026 · universe equal-weight: −7.0% then +51.1%

The filters are amplifying market direction, not predicting it. In the falling half the volume filter did worse than doing nothing (−2.76% vs −1.68%); in the rising half it did better (+4.67% vs +2.79%). That is the signature of beta, not alpha. Over a window that happened to rise 43%, beta looks exactly like skill.

What the data supports

  • 01
    Drop Open = Low and the gap-up rule.

    Neither beats the baseline on its own (p = 0.62 and p = 0.69), and removing Open = Low from the full set gives the best-supported combination in the whole study: 191 trades at +1.60%, p = 0.069. More than 13× the sample for a higher return.

  • 02
    Keep the volume rule — it is the one real find.

    1,692 trades at +1.86% against a +0.64% baseline, p < 0.001. It is the only filter here whose interval clears the baseline outright.

  • 03
    Keep the 3–5% band, with lower confidence.

    818 trades at +1.23%, p = 0.03. A genuine but modest effect, and it is the rule that turned the original strategy around: the earlier “under 3%” version returned −0.39% per trade.

  • 04
    Do not trade the four-rule set as specified.

    Not because it lost — it made +1.35% — but because 14 trades cannot tell you whether that is skill or luck, and the fragility test says luck. Two trades are the difference between +1.35% and negative.

  • 05
    None of it survives the regime test yet.

    Every filter, including the volume rule, was negative in the falling half of the window. Before trading any of this, it needs a multi-year history spanning at least one real drawdown.

Method

How the test was run

  • 01
    Volume benchmark excludes the signal day.

    The 30-session average is taken over the sessions before the signal, so a high-volume day cannot inflate its own benchmark. A symbol needs a full 30 sessions of history to be eligible — 6 otherwise-qualifying days were dropped for that reason, all in recently listed names.

  • 02
    Entry at the signal day's close; exits start the next session.

    Stop = signal-day low × 0.97, target = entry × 1.15. Because a 3–5% up day closes well above its open, real risk averages 6.08% from entry, not 3%.

  • 03
    Gaps exit at the open; a bar touching both levels is scored as a stop.

    No look-ahead in either direction, and the ambiguous case is resolved against the strategy. It never occurred in this window.

  • 04
    Confidence intervals are bootstrapped, p-values permuted.

    20,000 resamples for each. The p-value is the share of random same-size samples drawn from all 11,796 days that beat the subset's mean — a direct answer to “could this be chance?”

  • 05
    Overlaps, costs, and data integrity.

    One position per symbol gives 13 trades at +1.92% — same conclusion. Costs are excluded; Indian round-trip delivery costs run 0.3–0.6%. Prices are NSE's official bhavcopy, reconciled session by session: no missing sessions, no corporate actions in the window.

The limit that matters most: one year, 51 hand-picked names, and a market that rose 43%. That is enough to rank the four rules against each other, which is what this page does. It is not enough to certify any of them for live trading.
