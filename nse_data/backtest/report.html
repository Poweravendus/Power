The rules work exactly as designed. They just don't make money.

Seventy-four percent of trades hit the stop. The 15% target lands 20% of the time. Those two numbers very nearly cancel: the strategy needs a 25.2% win rate to break even and delivers 23.1%.

Read the other way — as a move measured from the open rather than the previous close — it lands on +0.10% per trade. Either way the result sits within a percentage point of zero. That is not a small edge; it is the absence of one.

Evidence 01

The Open = Low filter selects worse days than average

The obvious test: run the identical stop and target on every other day in the dataset. One catch — on an ordinary day the low sits further below the close, so “3% under the low” would hand non-signal days a wider stop and fewer stop-outs. To make it a fair fight, both sets get an identical stop distance from entry (4.63%, the signal set's own average).

Ordinary days beat the signal on all three measures. The filter isn't neutral — it costs you. A day that opens on its low and closes up less than 3% is, if anything, a marginally below-average day to buy.

Evidence 02

The stop is doing the damage, not the entry

Take the same 338 signals and remove the stop and target entirely — just hold for a fixed number of days. The signals turn profitable. The entry isn't the problem; the exit is.

Held 40 days with no stop, the same signals return +6.3% each and win 55% of the time. Bolting a 3% stop onto them converts that into −0.39%. The stop is not protecting the strategy — it is the strategy's largest cost.

Method

How the test was run

  • 01
    Rule 2 is ambiguous, so both readings were tested.

    “Below 3% up that day” can mean the move from the previous close (338 signals, −0.39%/trade) or the move from the open, which is also the low (331 signals, +0.10%/trade). Neither produces an edge, so the ambiguity doesn't change the conclusion.

  • 02
    Entry is the signal day's close; exits start the next session.

    Stop = signal-day low × 0.97. Target = entry × 1.15. Because the close is at or above the open, real risk averages 4.63%, not 3%.

  • 03
    Gaps exit at the open, not the level.

    A bar that opens through the stop fills at the open — worse than the stop. A bar that opens through the target fills at the open — better. No look-ahead in either direction.

  • 04
    A bar touching both levels is scored as a stop.

    Daily bars can't reveal intraday sequence, so the conservative reading is used. It never actually occurred here: no single bar spanned both a 4.6% loss and a 15% gain.

  • 05
    Overlapping signals were checked separately.

    Allowing only one live position per symbol gives 244 trades at −0.17%/trade (vs open: 250 trades at +0.37%). Same conclusion.

  • 06
    Costs are excluded.

    No brokerage, STT, exchange fees, GST, stamp duty, or slippage. Indian round-trip delivery costs run roughly 0.3–0.6%. Applied to a −0.39% expectancy over 338 trades, costs make a marginal result clearly negative.

  • 07
    Data is unadjusted, and verified not to need adjustment.

    Prices come from NSE's official security-wise bhavcopy. Every row's previous close was reconciled against the prior session's close: no corporate action affected any of these 51 names in the window, and no session is missing.

The honest limit of this test: one year, 51 hand-picked names, one market regime — and a strongly bullish one. That is enough to say the rules as specified have no edge here. It is not enough to say the underlying idea is dead in every regime, or that the wider-stop variants at the corner of the grid are real. Those need a longer history and a broader universe.
