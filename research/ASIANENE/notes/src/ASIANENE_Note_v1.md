## Company Overview

- Asian Energy Services (AESL) is a Mumbai-based energy services contractor with four lines of business: onshore seismic data acquisition for ONGC, Oil India, Vedanta and private explorers; operations and maintenance and, since July 2025, integrated field development for Vedanta's Cambay and Rajasthan fields; coal handling plant EPC for Coal India subsidiaries and SCCL, executed through unincorporated joint ventures; and, since September 2025, Kuiper Group, a Gulf and South-East Asian oilfield crew-supply business bought for Rs.123cr. It also owns a 50% participating interest in the small Indrora oil field in Gujarat, bought from its promoter. FY26 consolidated revenue was Rs.791cr, of which Rs.299cr was Kuiper's seven months.

- The company was incorporated in 1992 as Asian Oilfield Services, listed in 1995, spent 2008 to 2016 under Samara Capital with a loss-making Kurdistan venture, and has been controlled since FY17 by Oilmax Energy, the E&P company of Dr Kapil Garg, a former BG India managing director. Oilmax holds 56% after the May-2026 warrant conversion and is now being merged into AESL at 117 AESL shares for every 10 Oilmax shares, sanctioned by the NCLT on 29-Sep-2026. The merger brings in Oilmax's participating interests in five oil and gas blocks and a quartzite mine, and takes the promoter family's holding to 49.45% of an enlarged 8.80cr shares.

- FY26 revenue grew 70% but organic growth was 6%: Rs.299cr of the Rs.326cr increase came from Kuiper, which runs at a 7% EBITDA margin. Consolidated EBITDA margin fell to 12.1% from 25.5% in FY22; PAT was Rs.51.8cr; ROE 11.5%. Three-year operating cash flow is negative Rs.24cr against Rs.119cr of PAT, receivables plus unbilled work stand at 209 days of sales, and gross debt rose from Rs.24cr to Rs.158cr to fund Kuiper and Indrora wells. The standalone order book is Rs.1,754cr, of which the Vedanta integrated contract (Rs.772cr) and the MCL Lakhanpur coal handling plant (Rs.459cr) are two-thirds.

- At Rs.455 the stock trades at about 40x FY26 earnings and 21x FY26 EV/EBITDA, up from Rs.239 in March 2026 on the 1QFY27 revenue print, a Gujarat power EPC win and the merger sanction. No institution holds the stock; BSE turnover averages about Rs.1cr a day. The price discounts the order book, a 30 to 40% standalone growth guide and E&P optionality before any post-merger quarter has been reported.

## Revenue and EBITDA Margin - Business Cycles

In 34 years as a listed company AESL has never had a revenue base that survived the loss of one contract or one customer. Each of the five cycles below was carried by one or two awards and ended when they did. The audited major-customer note proves it: the top customer was 51 to 87% of revenue in every year from FY17 to FY21.


### Cycle 1 - FY05 to FY13: a small domestic seismic contractor

Revenue of Rs.8cr to Rs.69cr from 2D and 3D onshore seismic and shot-hole drilling for ONGC and small operators. Margins of 12 to 33% in the two good years (FY07 and FY08), then a 71% revenue fall in FY10 as work dried up and seven years of losses. Samara Capital bought control in 2008 and installed an ex-WesternGeco team in 2013. The FY14 annual report's own description of the home market still holds: "In India, the activity continued to be low and sporadic."

### Cycle 2 - FY14 to FY16: Kurdistan

One "mega contract" in Iraqi Kurdistan for Gazprom Neft, then a 3D award from Oil Search, took revenue to Rs.122cr in FY14 and Rs.141cr in FY15. The crew spread was bought for the job: Rs.108cr of capex in FY14, 7.7 times depreciation, funded partly by inter-corporate deposits at 15 to 16%. The December-2013 deck promised a "path to USD 75mm+ revenues". Oil collapsed, Kurdistan became a war zone and by FY17 the equipment was stranded in Sulaymaniyah and written off. EBITDA was negative in all three years, net worth fell from Rs.65cr to Rs.10cr and net debt reached Rs.101cr, ten times equity. Samara sold its 56% to Oilmax in 2016.

### Cycle 3 - FY17 to FY20: the Oilmax rebuild, which was really a Nigerian cycle

Oilmax put in Rs.137cr of equity over FY17 and FY18 through warrants at Rs.80, repaid the debt and wrote off the past (Rs.36cr of bad-debt charges in FY17, offset by a Rs.19cr write-back of payables booked as an exceptional gain). Revenue rose from Rs.124cr to Rs.273cr and the EBITDA margin from minus 15% to 24%. The decks described a domestic seismic story with ten crews and a Rs.503cr seismic book. The audited customer note shows what actually carried the numbers: one Nigerian customer each year. Koral Energy's FPU O&M contract was 57% of FY18 revenue before Koral terminated it in May 2018; Amni International's Langley turnaround project was 87% of FY20 revenue, the best year on record (PAT Rs.29cr, operating cash flow Rs.89cr, net cash Rs.82cr). Domestic seismic in the same years was interrupted by force majeure in Manipur and NC Hills, a Rs.12.5cr bank guarantee encashed by Oil India, and a USD 37m Bashneft Iraq order that never converted.

### Cycle 4 - FY21 to FY23: stall and collapse

Amni suspended Langley in November 2020 over its own technical problems and the contract was terminated in August 2021, leaving a USD 8.5m receivable that the auditor qualified for three years. Revenue held at Rs.229cr and Rs.260cr in FY21 and FY22 on the Vedanta seismic framework and the first coal handling plant and O&M awards, then fell 58% to Rs.110cr in FY23. The filings give the reasons: government seismic tendering stopped ("there has been no activity for the last 5-6 months in terms of the tendering", 1QFY23 call), the Oil India Tripura project was short-closed, the Hura coal plant waited for land, and the company kept the staff it had hired for new verticals. The oil and gas segment earned nothing on Rs.63cr of revenue; the year's loss was Rs.44cr. Investors on the August-2022 call were still working to a Rs.400 to 500cr revenue expectation.

### Cycle 5 - FY24 to FY26: the coal pivot and Kuiper

The board's March-2024 strategy statement set the direction: move "from cyclical business which also frequently involves force majeure situations, to stable business with long term contracts". Coal handling plants for Eastern, Central and Mahanadi Coalfields and SCCL took the mineral segment from Rs.47cr to Rs.273cr by FY25, 59% of revenue. In FY26 the mix flipped again: the mineral segment fell 42% between projects, while Kuiper's seven months and the start of the Vedanta integrated contract lifted oil and gas to Rs.633cr. Growth was funded by Rs.85cr of warrants in 2023, Rs.157cr of warrants in 2024, and Rs.158cr of debt at FY26. The company missed its FY26 standalone guide of 40 to 50% growth by delivering 6%.

### What the cycles teach

The present book depends on Vedanta (about 40%) and MCL (about 26%), which is the same two-contract structure as every prior cycle; the overseas leg is again a single acquired platform in a volatile region; and FY23 is the stress test for the cost base, because when awards stop the fixed cost of a standing organisation produces a loss within two quarters.

### 1. Seismic data acquisition - the original business, now 5% of the order book

Seismic crews record sound echoes to map the rock below a licence block before anyone drills. AESL fields a few hundred people per crew with recording channels, geophones and either shot-hole drilling or vibroseis trucks (commissioned in 2020 for Rajasthan). Contracts come from tenders by ONGC, Oil India, GAIL and private operators such as Sun Petrochemicals, are priced per line-kilometre of 2D or per square kilometre of 3D with mobilisation paid separately, and are billed monthly on certified progress. Recent awards show the scale: ONGC 3D Gujarat Rs.78cr over 11 months (2023), GAIL Rajasthan Rs.17cr over six months (2023), Oil India 2D Rajasthan Rs.82cr over 18 months (2024), Sun Petrochemicals 3D Rs.46cr over 12 months (2025) and 4D Rs.55cr over a year (2025).

The economics are those of a seasonal, labour-heavy service with its own equipment: no work in the monsoon, crews paid monthly whether or not the client has certified progress, force majeure stopping revenue but not cost, and a bank guarantee at risk on every job. Management puts seismic gross margins at 30 to 35% before depreciation on an equipment fleet that is now 74% depreciated: oilfield equipment of Rs.253cr gross carries Rs.66cr net, and FY26 additions were Rs.0.9cr. Seismic revenue was about Rs.120cr in FY24 and Rs.72cr in FY25; FY26 is not disclosed. ONGC put AESL on a tender holiday for six months to March 2025 "on certain technical grounds" relating to 2017 and 2018 contracts, never explained further; Oil India empanelled it for onshore 2D and 3D for three years in June 2026, which is eligibility rather than orders.

### 2. Oilfield O&M and integrated field development - the Vedanta relationship

Once a field produces, the operator needs people to run and maintain the plant around the clock. AESL's reference contract is Vedanta's Suvali onshore terminal and Cambay offshore platforms: Rs.248cr including GST over four years from April 2022, roughly Rs.50cr a year plus reimbursables, extended in March 2026 to March 2027 "on the same rates, terms and conditions". The step-up came in July 2025: a Rs.772cr (ex-GST) integrated services contract over 57 months for Vedanta's Rajasthan satellite fields, under which AESL drills new wells, builds facilities and then operates the field. The pricing, per management, reimburses capex at the bid number and pays opex per barrel on baseline and incremental production; AESL takes no output or oil-price risk. Smaller work of the same kind runs for Hardy at the PY-3 offshore field (where a weather-driven cost claim was never recognised), for Oilmax at Amguri in Assam, and under a three-year build-own-operate-transfer contract for Assam Gas Company's compressor station (about Rs.200cr, Rs.82cr in year one).

This is where the company's only durable customer relationship sits: Vedanta has been a seismic buyer since 2019, an O&M customer since 2022, and has now given AESL its largest contract. It is also where concentration risk sits, because the Rs.772cr award is about 40% of the order book and Vedanta's own operated production fell 16% in FY26.

### 3. Coal handling plants - the growth engine of FY24 and FY25

At an opencast mine, raw coal has to be crushed, conveyed, stored in silos and loaded into rail wagons quickly. Coal India's "first-mile connectivity" programme has tendered these plants since 2020 to move coal by rail rather than road. AESL builds them as turnkey EPC jobs, usually with a five-year O&M tail, and in one case (Eastern Coalfields' Hura plant, Rs.128cr) on a build-own-operate-transfer basis. Awards since 2022: Hura (ECL, Rs.128cr), Gevra for HEC (Rs.236cr, as sub-contractor), Karo (Central Coalfields, Rs.250cr with five-year O&M), Singareni's PKOCP conveyors (Rs.148cr plus GST, 18 months), Rajmahal plough feeders (Rs.22cr), MCL Lakhanpur (Rs.459cr including GST over seven years, won November 2025, the largest in the company's history) and, in June 2026, the first order outside Coal India: GSECL's Ukai thermal station, Rs.188cr including GST over two to three years.

Two features matter for the numbers. First, Coal India tenders require specific track records, so AESL bids through unincorporated joint ventures with partners who hold the qualification (Zuberi, FFIL, Indwell, Global); AESL books "sale of services" to the JV, the JV bills the mine, and the JV is equity-accounted. Related-party revenue through these JVs and Oilmax was 51% of consolidated revenue in FY25 and 30% in FY26, and Rs.91cr of AESL's receivables at FY26 were owed by its own JVs. Second, revenue is recognised on percentage of completion, which the new auditor made its key audit matter: unbilled contract assets stood at Rs.164cr at FY26, up from Rs.86cr, and retention money held by customers at Rs.95cr, up from Rs.54cr. Cost is bought-in steel, structures and equipment (stores and consumables were Rs.212cr in FY25) plus sub-contracted civil work, so the margin is protected when a quarter washes out but the cash is slow. Management puts coal plant margins at 20 to 23% and the win rate above 50%. No commissioning has been announced for Hura (awarded February 2022), Singareni or Gevra despite three sets of promised dates.

### 4. Kuiper - a Gulf crew-supply business, 38% of revenue at a 7% margin

Kuiper Group recruits, employs, visas and deploys skilled oilfield workers, mainly to offshore drilling rigs and EPC contractors in Saudi Arabia, Qatar, the UAE, Oman, Egypt and South-East Asia, under master service agreements of two to five years. AESL bought it from Gulf Capital in a deal announced in April 2025 and closed on 29-Aug-2025: USD 9.25m of consideration, Rs.87.6cr at the SPA price plus Rs.35.3cr of working-capital true-ups, a net investment of Rs.122.8cr. The business came with Rs.72cr of cash and Rs.163cr of fair-valued net assets, so the accounts show a Rs.40cr bargain-purchase gain in equity rather than goodwill. Revenue was USD 82m in 2022, USD 74m in 2023 and USD 68m in 2024, the fall reflecting Aramco's suspension of some 20 jackup rigs in 2024; management's run-rate is Rs.40 to 45cr a month, USD 60 to 65m for FY27, and a target of USD 100m by FY29 or FY30.

The economics are those of a payroll pass-through: crew salary and related cost of Rs.256cr against Rs.299cr of revenue in FY26, a gross margin of about 14%, EBITDA of about 7%, net of about 6% in low-tax jurisdictions. It is asset-light but working-capital heavy, with monthly billing at 60 to 90 days. It is the reason consolidated margin fell to 12.1% in FY26 and to 8.1% in 1QFY27, and it ties the group to Gulf rig counts and, as the March-2026 Qatar disruption showed, to the region's politics. Kuiper's standalone profit is not disclosed as such; the entity table in the annual report shows its Dubai company, OCB Oilfield Services DMCC, as the single largest profit contributor in the group at Rs.34cr before consolidation eliminations.

### 5. Owning a slice of an oil field - Indrora, and what the merger adds

In 2023 AESL bought a 50% participating interest in the Indrora discovered small field in Gujarat from Oilmax, the operator, for Rs.15cr upfront plus production-linked payments. The press release of the time targeted 300 barrels a day by December 2023 and a potential 700. Production was about 100 barrels a day in September 2025 and 150 to 200 in May 2026; the new target is 1,000 by FY27. The Mevad NM-01 well drilled in January 2026 tested at about 100 barrels a day. AESL has Rs.82cr invested in the interest at FY26 (Rs.36cr of oil assets and Rs.46cr of wells in progress) and has committed about Rs.50cr more for six wells. FY26 crude sales were Rs.5cr. After the merger, field ownership becomes a third segment with Oilmax's Amguri (50%, producing), Duarmara (50%) and Tiphuk (80%) in Assam, South Rewa CBM (100%) in Chhattisgarh, a Kutch shallow-offshore block won in DSF-IV in August 2026, and a quartzite mine in Uttarakhand.

### Where the money comes from, and who owes it

Three customers were each more than 10% of FY26 revenue; the top customer was Rs.143cr (18%) and the top three Rs.264cr (33%), down from 24% and 53% in FY25 only because Kuiper added a spread of new clients. Names are not disclosed, but the pattern across years points to Vedanta and the coal-plant JVs. Payment terms are 30 to 60 days except retention, which is released at project completion. The group's receivables and unbilled work of Rs.512cr at FY26 break into Rs.164cr of unbilled contract assets, Rs.95cr of retention, Rs.91cr owed by AESL's own JVs, Rs.10.5cr owed by Oilmax, Rs.22cr fully provided (mostly the Amni balance, now more than three years old) and the balance in normal terms.

## Industry and Competition

### Onshore seismic: a shrinking, two-contractor market with the biggest buyer going in-house

India acquired about 15,700 square kilometres of 3D and 2,400 line-kilometres of 2D seismic in FY24 across all operators, most of it ONGC's and most of ONGC's acquired by its own crews. ONGC's 3D acquisition halved in FY26 to 4,631 square kilometres while its 2D rose to 957 line-kilometres; swings of 40 to 60% a year in the largest buyer's volumes are normal. The government programmes that created onshore demand are finishing: the National Seismic Programme (48,000 line-kilometres of 2D) completed acquisition in February 2022, and Mission Anveshan (20,275 line-kilometres across seven onland basins, Rs.792cr outlay) was due to complete in FY26; AESL's Rs.82cr Oil India Rajasthan contract is part of it. The Rs.84,084cr Samudra Manthan offshore exploration scheme approved in July 2026, including Rs.28,534cr for seismic, is offshore and deepwater, where AESL has no vessel, crew or track record; those contracts have gone to Shearwater and TGS.

Triangulating the outsourced onshore market from the two listed contractors' revenue and the named awards of the last 30 months gives Rs.250 to 400cr a year, against the "about Rs.1,000cr" management has cited in annual reports. The only listed peer, Alphageo, saw revenue fall 85% from its FY18 peak of Rs.429cr to Rs.66cr in FY23 and is loss-making in FY25 and FY26 on Rs.112cr of revenue. Chinese land contractors (BGP, Sinopec) are effectively excluded from PSU tenders by the 2020 procurement rule on bidders from countries sharing a land border; Western land contractors are absent. That leaves AESL, Alphageo, ONGC's in-house parties and Oil India's own crews. ONGC bought three Sercel recording systems with 24,000 channels in October 2025 for the 2026 season, which is the clearest signal that the in-house share will rise. AESL's own stated win rate in seismic tenders is one in three.

### Coal handling plants: a real programme, smaller and lumpier than the headline

The Ministry of Coal's first-mile-connectivity programme has grown from 55 projects in 2023 to 139 by July 2026, with 72 commissioned (about 589 million tonnes a year of capacity) and a target of 1,319 million tonnes by FY30, at a total capex of about Rs.31,000cr including rail sidings and silos. Coal India's capex has plateaued at Rs.16,000 to 20,000cr a year (FY26 Rs.19,607cr; FY27 budget Rs.16,500cr), with evacuation infrastructure earmarked at about Rs.5,600cr in FY26; in the first quarter of FY27 it spent Rs.195cr on plants and silos and Rs.754cr on rail links. The realistic award pool for the plant EPC that AESL does is therefore Rs.1,500 to 3,000cr a year, awarded in lumps, not the Rs.4,000cr a year the headline implies, and management's "Rs.20,000cr to be awarded in five years" is the top of the range. AESL's roughly Rs.1,170cr of plant awards since 2022 is about 4% of the programme's value and makes it, on the public record, the most frequent winner of recent plant tenders; the competitor set it names is small (Samanta, Madhucon, its own partner Indwell, L&T on larger jobs), and two of the large EPC houses of the 2010s, Tecpro and McNally Bharat, are defunct. Demand beyond Coal India is opening (SCCL, GSECL's Ukai order, private power producers), and the official policy requires mechanised handling within five years at mines above 2 million tonnes a year. The programme tails off after FY29 unless the project count keeps being revised up, as it has three times.

### Competition and bargaining power

PSU contracts are won by technical pre-qualification followed by lowest price; private operators negotiate and repeat-award. AESL wins where it has a reference job and a partner, and loses where it needs a vessel or a Chinese cost base. Supplier power is real in recording systems (a Sercel, INOVA and Wireless Seismic oligopoly that will sell directly to ONGC) and shared with JV partners in coal plants. Customer power shows in the balance sheet rather than in disclosed terms: retention rose from Rs.54cr to Rs.95cr in a year when standalone revenue grew 6%, and CRISIL measured receivable days at 284 in September 2025.

## Business Model, Value Chain and Moat

AESL sits as a tier-one contractor to oil companies in seismic and O&M, a JV lead billing a joint venture in coal plants, and a labour supplier to drilling contractors through Kuiper. People are 41% of FY26 cost (crew salaries Rs.256cr, project employees Rs.28cr, other employees Rs.42cr); bought-in materials and equipment for coal plants were Rs.170cr; sub-contracting Rs.121cr; fuel is immaterial. Input inflation sits with AESL on fixed-price seismic and lump-sum EPC work, with the client on the Vedanta integrated contract (capex reimbursed, opex per barrel) and on Kuiper's cost-plus day rates. There is no proprietary technology; the wireless-seismic edge of 2013 to 2020 has not been renewed.

The moat is thin and mostly contract-specific. Pre-qualification takes two to four years and one completed reference job per category, and coal-plant qualification is held through partners an entrant could also hire. The Vedanta relationship is the one asset that took years to build. Equipment ownership is decaying (74% depreciated, Rs.0.9cr of additions). Regulatory protection is nil. The weakest layer is people: the chief operating officer lasted nine months to July 2024, the head of infrastructure projects left in July 2025, and the heads of seismic, O&M and procurement resigned between 31-Aug and 13-Sep-2026, just before the merger took effect, with no disclosed successors. A well-funded entrant could replicate the stack in three to five years, and could buy a Kuiper-type platform for what AESL paid.

## Preliminary View

The market is paying a growth multiple for a business whose growth is order-book driven, working-capital hungry and equity funded, and it is paying it before the merger's dilution appears in a reported quarter. The evidence says the business is better than its capital allocation record and worse than its price.

- **Every cycle has rested on one or two contracts, and this one does too.** The Vedanta integrated contract and MCL Lakhanpur are two-thirds of the Rs.1,754cr book. The top customer has been 18 to 87% of revenue in every year since FY17. When the Nigerian contract stopped in FY21 and tendering stopped in FY23, the cost base did not flex: revenue fell Rs.150cr and EBITDA fell Rs.85cr. The company has not shown it can absorb the loss of a large contract.

- **Profit has not become cash in the growth years.** PAT of Rs.119cr over FY24 to FY26 against operating cash flow of minus Rs.24cr; FY26's reported Rs.53cr includes Rs.20cr of fixed-deposit redemptions and Kuiper's opening working capital. Receivables plus unbilled work are 209 days of sales. Since FY17 shareholders have put in Rs.270cr (and Rs.92cr more in May 2026), received Rs.4.4cr in dividends, and seen the share count double before the merger doubles it again. The incremental pre-tax return on the Rs.406cr of capital added since FY20 is 8.6%.

- **The merger brings in a promoter company whose earnings have already fallen.** The 117-for-10 swap was set in September 2025 on a DCF value of Rs.4,349 per Oilmax share, about Rs.2,480cr, using FY25 earnings of Rs.48.6cr. Oilmax reported a Rs.12.2cr loss for the nine months to December 2025 on revenue down to Rs.72cr, after a Rs.34cr ESOP charge. Existing public holders' claim on AESL's own earnings falls from about 44% to about 24% of the enlarged company; Oilmax must earn about Rs.42cr a year for the swap to be neutral to them on FY26 numbers. Tiphuk and Duarmara, promised for FY26, have slipped to FY27.

- **What is genuinely good.** A coal-plant franchise with few credible competitors and a better-than-50% win rate; a Vedanta relationship that has grown for seven years and has produced the company's largest contract; Kuiper bought at 0.75x book with Rs.72cr of cash inside; a clean FY26 audit with no contingent liabilities; a promoter who has put in money three times. The bear case has to respect that there is a business here.

**What would make this view wrong.** Standalone revenue growth of 30% or more in H1FY27 with operating cash flow positive before treasury movements and receivable days below about 170; and Oilmax FY26 audited profit before ESOP cost of Rs.40cr or more with Tiphuk or Duarmara producing by March 2027. **What would confirm it.** A further equity raise within twelve months of the May-2026 conversion, or a second consecutive Oilmax loss.

The rating and target price come with the forecast model in v2. The model has to answer: what revenue the committed contracts support in FY27 to FY29 contract by contract; how much cash 30% growth absorbs at 120 to 130 working-capital days and whether debt rises; what Kuiper is worth at USD 55 to 65m and 7 to 9%; what production Oilmax needs to earn Rs.42cr and what its blocks are worth on a NAV basis; and what multiple a 12 to 14% margin contractor with 12% ROE deserves on 8.80cr shares.

What comes in: participating interests in Amguri (50%, producing about 1,850 barrels of oil equivalent a day at the group level), Duarmara (50%) and Tiphuk (80%), both "expected production in FY26" in the September-2025 deck and now FY27, South Rewa CBM (100%, under development), a Kutch shallow-offshore DSF-IV block (50%, operator, awarded August 2026), a quartzite mine, and "about 70 million barrels" of claimed reserves with no certified report in the public record. Management's target is Oilmax revenue of Rs.800 to 900cr and 10,000 barrels a day by FY30, from about 2,500 today.

What it does to the economics: at Rs.455 the market pays about Rs.3,030cr for the 6.67cr new shares, of which about Rs.1,240cr is the cancelled stake and about Rs.1,790cr the E&P business. Public holders of AESL go from a 44% claim on AESL's earnings to a 24% claim on the combined company; the swap is neutral to them only if Oilmax earns about Rs.42cr a year, which it did in FY25 and did not in the nine months since. The merger also removes a layer: the Rs.27.8cr of services AESL sold to Oilmax in FY26, the Oilmax guarantees on AESL's bank lines and the Indrora operatorship all become intra-group, which is the right structure even if the price of admission is dilution. It also takes the promoter below 50%.

- **Three peaks, three drawdowns.** Rs.446 in January 2008 on a 15-month year with Rs.9.6cr of profit and a Rs.40cr raise; the price took sixteen years to be seen again. Each peak was followed by a 65 to 85% fall within two years, which is why long-term holders have been trained to sell re-ratings.

- **The best price coincided with the best numbers.** At the March-2020 low of Rs.62 the company had Rs.82cr of net cash, Rs.29cr of profit and a P/E of 9x. The market was still pricing the FY18 collapse.

- **The current leg.** Rs.239 in March 2026 to Rs.455 in October: the 1QFY27 print (revenue up 135% on Kuiper), the GSECL order, the Mevad discovery and the merger sanction. A Rs.2,200cr company priced by a handful of marginal buyers can move 30% either way on one quarter.

## Triggers to Watch

- **Record date and listing of the merger shares (October to November 2026).** Confirms the share count and the promoter's sub-50% holding, and whether a doubled public float brings supply.

- **H1FY27 results (November 2026).** Standalone growth against the 30 to 40% guide; consolidated margin against 12 to 13%; operating cash flow before treasury movements; receivable plus unbilled days at September 2026. This is the first test of the view.

- **First post-merger segment disclosure (likely 3QFY27, February 2027) and Oilmax's FY26 audited accounts.** Whether Oilmax earns the Rs.42cr a year the swap needs, and whether the nine-month loss was ESOP alone.

- **First oil at Tiphuk and Duarmara (guided for Q1FY27, already slipped) and the Amguri gas connection.** The production ramp the merger valuation assumes.

- **Commissioning of Hura, Singareni and Gevra coal plants and release of retention (Rs.95cr) by March 2027.** Whether projects complete and cash follows.

- **CRISIL's resolution of "Watch Developing" after the merger, and any board notice of a preferential or warrant issue.** An independent view of the enlarged balance sheet; or the dilution pattern repeating.

## Key Risks

- **Oilmax earns less than the swap assumes.** The merger was valued on FY25 profit of Rs.48.6cr; the nine months to December 2025 show a Rs.12.2cr loss and both new fields have slipped a year. Below about Rs.42cr of Oilmax profit the swap dilutes existing holders' earnings, and the enlarged share count is permanent. We would know from the first post-merger segment numbers and the FY26 Oilmax accounts.

- **Cash does not follow profit and equity is raised again.** Rs.512cr of receivables and unbilled work, Rs.95cr of it retention and Rs.91cr owed by the company's own JVs, against Rs.158cr of new debt; a ten-year record of funding growth with equity. A further raise below today's price would confirm the pattern. We would know from H1FY27 cash flow and any board notice.

- **Kuiper's rig-count exposure and the Gulf.** A 7%-margin crew-supply business that lost 17% of its revenue in 2024 when Aramco parked rigs, and was disrupted in Qatar in March 2026, now 38% of consolidated revenue with a SOFR-linked loan behind it. We would know from the gap between consolidated and standalone revenue each quarter.

## Final Outlook

::: key Rating: Under review  |  Target Price: v2  |  Preliminary view: cautious  (CMP Rs.455, market cap Rs.2,211cr; about Rs.4,000cr post merger)

Asian Energy Services enters FY27 as a different company from the one that reported FY25: Kuiper makes it 38% international at a third of the margin, the Vedanta integrated contract makes it a field developer rather than a maintenance contractor, and the Oilmax merger makes it an oil producer with a promoter who no longer holds a majority. The order book of Rs.1,754cr is real and the coal-plant franchise has few competitors. Those are the reasons the stock has doubled since March.

The reasons for caution are in the record rather than the story. Each of the company's five cycles was carried by one or two contracts and ended when they did; the current book has the same shape. Profit has not turned into cash in any of the last three years, growth has been paid for with equity in every cycle, and the share count is about to double again at a swap ratio set before the merging company turned loss-making. At 40x trailing earnings the price assumes that the FY27 guide is met, that Kuiper's margin improves and that Oilmax's fields produce on schedule, and none of the three has yet appeared in a reported quarter.

The next two results will settle most of this. H1FY27 in November shows whether standalone growth and cash conversion are on track; the first post-merger quarter shows what Oilmax contributes. Until then the honest position is that the services business is worth owning at a price that reflects a 12 to 14% margin contractor with slow collections, and that the merger is an unpriced variable with a wide range. The forecast model in v2 will put numbers and a rating on both.
